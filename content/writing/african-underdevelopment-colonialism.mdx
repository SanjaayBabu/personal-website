Scholars debate about the reasons for Africa's failed economic development who cite a few main "buckets" of reasons—historical, economic, cultural, naturalist, and developmental. I theorize that the main reason that underlies them all is that Africa still has not moved on from its legacy of colonialism. Rather, colonial institutions and policies persist, and the discourse of development have created a form of neocolonialism.

Colonialism underlies the arguments, made by economists, that Africa has failed at manufacturing and industrialization. Colonial-era taxation policies, geared towards resource extraction, sought to configure African economies such that it would produce ONLY low-value added, unprocessed raw materials at the cheapest possible prices. This created economies such as that of the Sahel—single-crop, single-industry, with no high value-added manufacturing. Post-independence, when African countries sought to indigenize high value-added production, such as cotton refining, they faced tariff barriers from Western nations, cuffing industrialization efforts—this was the argument made by the presidents of various African nations who were interviewed for Basil Davidson’s “Magnificent African Cake.” Having failed to industrialize, African nations continue to primarily export low value-added goods—thus gaining little revenue. African nations are also susceptible to inflation in the prices for high value-added goods, diminishing their purchasing power. Thus, colonial-era taxation policies, coupled with tariff policies from Western former colonial powers, have left Africa unable to industrialize, harming its GDP, purchasing power, and in some cases, causing currency devaluation.

Others argue that a distrustful culture and weak institutions belie Africa's inability to develop economically. I would argue that this, too, stems from colonialism in Africa. Indirect rule policies systematically exploited tensions and rivalries between tribes and stoked hatred, so that the divided African continent would be easier to govern. One notable example we discussed in class was how Belgian rulers favored the Tutsi people over the Hutu in Rwanda—the Tutsi were seen as more Caucasian and thus more fit to rule. The Hutu-Tutsi rivalry manufactured by the Belgians persisted long after independence, to the point of causing a genocide in the 1990s. The ethnic hatred created by colonial powers influences Africans' preference to interact—socially and economically—with their own ethnicity and tribe, and diminishes trust. Weak institutions, too, have arisen due to colonial-era governance. Corruption that was rife during indirect rule continues today; and, according to Basil Davidson's film, post-independence regimes were often a continuation of colonial regimes, such as Mukasa's rule in Uganda. Africans have thus maintained their distrust of strong governments, distrust that was built during the colonial period. Colonialism is thus the underlying factor behind distrust and weak institutions that have inhibited African economic growth.

Some detractors would counter my argument by claiming that many former colonies, such as China, India and Singapore, have managed to develop. Why can't Africa do the same? I have a response to each:

China is far more ethnically homogenous and they have a longer history of being a unified state. Post-colonial governance was thus not wrought with the tribal tensions / "pan-Africanism debate" that Africa faced.

India is indeed diverse. Yet, the independence "struggle" against the British unified Indians under a common narrative, and called for a clean break from colonial-era institutions and practices. Indeed, they are less dependent, politically and economically, on their colonial powers. In Africa, though, independence was often "granted" by colonial powers to earn the trust and support of Africans. Indeed, many post-independence leaders were chosen for their loyalty to colonial masters, and thus, they preserved colonial-era institutions. Resultingly, Africa has not had a "clean break" from imperialism that India had.

In Singapore's case, after independence, they were allowed to put up high tariff barriers, so that imports would become expensive, allowing domestic industrialization and capital formation. This is also known as Import-Substitution Industrialization. When Africa tried to put up similar tariff barriers, the West disallowed this, using IMF Structural Adjustment packages to force Africa to remove tariff barriers. The West did not want Africa to industrialize—African industrialization would eliminate a huge market for Western final goods, and reduce the West's supply of cheap raw materials.

For these reasons, comparing Africa to China, India or Singapore is not sound. Colonialism affected (and continues to affect) Africa in much more prolonged and pernicious ways than it did China, India and Singapore.

I have proven that colonialism is the foundational reason behind economic, cultural and developmentalist views of Africa's failed economic development. I will add that colonialism continues to affect Africa today, due to the "discourse" of Development—as argued by Escobar. Today, due to narratives that Africa is poor and is in need of saving by the West and China, many Africans are dependent on said nations. Conditional western aid and the accompanying Washington Consensus conditions, as explained by Harvey, limit African sovereignty and autonomy—however, they cannot do without aid, and have no choice but to follow. Chinese aid comes with its own risks—commentators fear Chinese "debt-trapping" that might constrain Africa's ability to use its resources, its only source of economic power now. With its dependent attitude, Africans are unwilling to help themselves and one another—Bornstein's argument in the context of child sponsorship shows how richer Africans / Zimbabweans don't really help their own due to their thinking of "why should I help if the Westerners are helping?" This leaves Africa fully dependent—a form of neo-colonialism. While colonialism has ended, a new form of colonialism has taken its place through the Foucauldian discourse of development, as argued by Escobar.

My essay conclusively proves that colonialism and neocolonialism underlie economic, developmentalist and cultural explanations for Africa's failed economic development. I will, however, end on a more positive note. Africa looks poised to move on from neocolonial dependency. Pharmaceuticals, according to Karrar Karrar, is one sign. Realizing that imported medicine is unreliable due to vaccine nationalism, Africans have moved to address material, regulatory and epistemic gaps. Perhaps if Africans succeed at indigenizing pharma manufacturing through pan-Africanism and South-South collaboration, Africa can see new momentum to break from its colonial past that's holding it back, and achieve renewed industrialization, allowing the African economy to soar.
