# Chinese aid in Africa: pros and cons

It’s well-recognized that China has made a strong push towards Africa. China is not alone in this journey. Many emerging powers including India and Brazil have also invested in Africa. Why?

There are three potential reasons why a country would invest so much into the development of other countries.

1. Natural resources: Development sometimes comes with preferential access to natural resources.
2. Economic markets: Development efforts increases the disposable income in target countries. It also engenders the kind of “goodwill” that would encourage citizens of target countries to buy goods and services from the donating country. This evidently plays out in Africa: it’s often considered a consumer economy. 
3. Soft power / political influence: During the Cold War, the two competing superpowers were vying for the support of so-called “third-world countries”, so that they could broaden their influence. In the present day, this could take the form of support in votes at multilateral organizations like the UN, though the UN itself is declining in influence. 

These are big drivers of aid into developing countries. It’s not often just out of goodwill. 

How is Chinese aid different from what we’ve seen before? For one, Chinese aid is often unconditional. While the Washington Consensus in the 1980s established a set of conditions for aid — including liberalizing markets, privatizing industry, etc. — the Chinese had a “Beijing Consensus” which rejects such conditions. Second, Chinese aid focusses less on building markets and institutions, but rather sees the building of tangible infrastructure. Examples include the suspension bridges, like one I crossed while in Nepal, and ports. 

So what if Chinese aid looks different? Chinese aid is seen by African countries as friendlier and more impactful. This means that broadly, the Chinese enjoy a good public perception in Africa.

The above graph describes a study done by Afrobarometer, an organization which surveys citizens of African countries to understand their sentiments on a range of issues. On the surface, it seems that China isn’t that much more popular amongst Africans compared to the Americans. However, there are some major in-group differences (all quoted from Afrobarometer’s report):

- In 16 of the 34 countries, China’s influence is more widely seen as positive than that of the U.S., including a 36-percentage-point gap in Eswatini (82% positive for China, 46% for the U.S.), a 25-point gap in Mauritius (75% vs. 50%), and a 19-point gap in Mali (81% vs. 62%) (Figure 7).
- The U.S. is more widely perceived as having positive influence than China in 10 countries, but Zimbabwe is the only country with a two-digit gap (48% for the U.S. vs. 38% for China).

If you’re curious, you can read the full report [here](https://www.afrobarometer.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/ad489-pap3-africans_welcome_chinas_influence_maintain_democratic_aspirations-afrobarometer_dispatch-15nov21.pdf). 

That said, some commentators see a dark side to Chinese aid. The word “debt-trapped diplomacy” is often brought up when describing Chinese aid in Africa in the context of the trillion-dollar Belt and Road Initiative (BRI). Their argument, in essence, is that China provides huge amounts of loans, and when African countries are unable to pay off their debt, they’re asked to provide preferential access to infrastructure, or are subjected to extractive raw material deals. 

So, zooming out, is Chinese aid any better? It depends. My personal view is that it doesn’t address the root issue that is slowing African development — challenges at achieving import-substitution industrialization. Due to a range of reasons, Africa continues to *export* low value-added goods and services, while importing higher value-added goods and services. This constantly leaves Africa vulnerable to changes in import prices or the cost of export goods. 

Ultimately, to promote African development, more countries need to allow Africa to industrialize. How? Perhaps the development story of Japan is telling. The US allowed Japan to put up tariff barriers so that it could engage in Import-Substitution Industrialization. It also absorbed Japanese final goods and services, buying almost 40% of all of Japanese exports. This kickstarted the Japanese economy. In the modern day, however, Africa doesn’t enjoy this level of flexibility — including from China. Therefore, I’m not optimistic that Chinese aid is going to do much better. 

# Fossil fuels: Incentives to innovate?

In our class about climate science, we learned about fossil fuels. We learnt about how the three major forms of fossil fuels — coal, oil, and natural gas — are formed and extracted. 

One specific process I found interesting was fracking. Fracking is essentially the reason why the US has found a huge new store of natural gas, and one of the technological innovations that has prevented us from running out of petroleum. Fracking taps on the natural gas that’s caught between rocks and isn’t very porous. Essentially, large amounts of sand and water are injected into “perforations” in horizontal fracking pipes deep down in the ground. This tremendously increases the pressure and fractures (”fracks”) the rocks, allowing natural gas to flow into the fracking pipes. We watched a useful video which you can find [here](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jUrGomRLnUA&pp=ygUyYW1lcmljYW4gcGV0cm9sZXVtIGluc3RpdHV0ZSBmcmFja2luZyBob3cgaXQgd29ya3M%3D).

The most interesting part of this fracking story, to me, is the degree of human ingenuity in innovation. When the profit incentive works right, the human spirit seems to be able to find solutions to the hardest problems. 

So my next question is, how do we create the right incentives for sustainable energy production? I’ve often thought about this question. It’s something I need to spend more time thinking about. I’d love to hear your ideas.

Nuclear power seems to be the next big frontier in energy. Data centers have created a huge demand for energy; in fact, in parts of the US, this is creating a huge uproar, because high energy use by data centers have resulted in energy prices surging in rural parts of the US where most data centers have located. 

With the rise of AI, is it possible that AI data centers become the biggest user of energy? If so, should we align profit incentives for tech/AI and energy? [David Friedberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Friedberg), a regular in the All-In Podcast, said in an episode I listened to that perhaps AI companies should build their own energy plants and pump energy *into* the grid. Part of AI profit-sharing could be that AI companies provide free energy to the communities they’re in. 

Friedberg sees this as an important way to mitigate and manage public resentment towards the raging success of tech companies. Such an energy-sharing initiative, to him, is akin to how Rockefeller, Carnegie, Morgan and Vanderbilt engaged in numerous “public relations” initiatives like building libraries, funding schools, and more, to mitigate public resentment towards their wealth. This is an interesting idea. I’d love to hear your thoughts!
