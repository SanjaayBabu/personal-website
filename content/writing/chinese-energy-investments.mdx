If we think about the vexed problem of African economic underdevelopment as a half-baked
pie, there are a few key missing ingredients in our pie. First, an economy that primarily exports low
value-added goods, but consumes high value-added imports in large quantities. Second, an
overdependence on foreign capital and aid, as persuasively argued by Moyo (2009). Finally, political
economy failings: vicious corruption and lack of state capacity, and a weak social contract between the
people and the government. Cumulatively, these have left the African economic pie half-baked.

Sengupta (2025) describes Chinese investment in African green energy infrastructure, primarily
through exporting cheap solar panels and bidding for contracts to establish solar farms. To analyze the
effects of these investments, we must analyze whether they mitigate or, to the contrary, exaggerate
these three failings. In this essay, I argue that Chinese investment is valuable but fails to address the
long-term structural flaws in the African economy, potentially even worsening them. I conclude by
offering two modifications to the Chinese investments that could allow these investments to sweeten
the pie in the short run and also refine and enlarge it in the long run.

# Pros: Quality of Life, Capital Expenditure

An evident benefit of Chinese energy investment in Africa is that it improves the quality of life
and services for Africans. Sengupta opens with an anecdote of how a dentist, Ismet Booley, initially
struggled with providing reliable dental services due to energy shortages and managed to address this
issue through installing cheap Chinese solar panels. In the short run, energy investments tangibly
improve Africans’ quality of life – they can enjoy reliable healthcare, education and other essential
services. As such, Chinese investment in African energy networks is similar to other forms of Chinese
Belt and Road Initiative aid that we discussed in class – such as road construction, and bridge building.

In the long run, such energy infrastructure could improve Africa’s economic potential. How?
First, money that was used for energy can now be redirected into more productive uses. Sengupta describes the use of solar panels in South Africa’s Lanzerac Wine Estate, and how it significantly lowers
energy costs. Rather than paying for energy, business revenue could be used to purchase new machinery
or hire new workers, all of which helps increase the output of African enterprises. Within the growth
framework of Moyo (2009), long-term investments spark growth. Second, energy investments are
long-term investments – even after the Chinese leave, the solar farms they constructed and the energy
grid they created will remain. In the article, the South African deputy energy minister emphasizes that
the infrastructure is owned by and remains under the control of the state. These investments are akin to
the industrial parks presented in the documentary Made in Ethiopia – the factory spaces and
infrastructure can be used by African businesses even if the Chinese choose to leave. With their long
time-horizon, energy investments establish the foundations for future African industrialization.

# Cons: Weakened African state & Dependency Theory

In spite of these benefits, however, Chinese investments in African energy infrastructure fail to
address, and even exaggerate, the structural issues in African economies – namely, an overdependence
on foreign capital, failed industrialization, and weak political institutions.

First, Chinese investment in energy further weakens the already weak African state. Sengupta
describes a “death spiral”, where well-off customers switch to solar, causing the state-run energy
provider Eskom to lose money and increase rates. As rates rise, more consumers switch to solar, further
harming Eskom. In enabling such “death spirals”, Chinese energy investments fill the void of what the
state should be expected to provide, further weakening the social contract between the people and the
state. Chinese solar investments decentralize energy production — traditionally a sector with high
economies of scale — exacerbating Africa’s informalization and further weakening the state.

What are the impacts of this? In the short run, there is an even weaker impetus for taxation,
because public institutions are no longer providing basic energy services. In weakening the case for
taxation, Chinese investments erode a critical check and balance between the government and people.
Moyo (2009) argues that taxation creates a greater sense of accountability compared to aid, because
people expect something constructive out of their tax revenue — she argues that taxation might reduce
corruption. To the contrary, Chinese investments might ostensibly worsen corruption. While Sengupta
doesn’t go far enough to assert the presence of corruption in the bidding process, there is a real
possibility that Chinese corporations might pay off officials for preferential treatment in establishing
build-operate contracts. In the long run, by eroding taxation, Chinese investment impedes the
government’s ability to engage in centralized industrial policy or investment. As argued by Cockburn
(2006) and Moyo (2009), industrial policy similar to that of the Asian Tiger economies is a necessary
ingredient for sustained economic progress. A lack of tax revenue and a weaker role for the government
in people’s lives prevents African governments from engaging in such projects, worsening African
nations’ odds of enjoying sustained development. The pie is worse off – Africa’s already-weak political
economy ends up even weaker, and Africa’s ability to engage in industrial policy is further diminished.

In addition to further harming the already-weak African states, Chinese energy investments
create new dependencies. Franke and Chasin (1981) define dependencies as “a set of political and
economic relationships that render the dependent country relatively powerless to control or affect their
development”. Sengupta’s article reveals two new dependencies created by Chinese investments in
South African infrastructure: dependency on consuming high value-added imports, and dependency on
foreign capital for investments. The former is clear in the case study of Colin Mkosi, the owner of a
bicycle delivery service – his electric bikes are purchased from China, and to power the bikes, he has
to purchase even more Chinese goods in the form of solar panels. China is creating an “ecosystem” of
consumer goods, making it difficult for African countries to wean off their dependency on Chinese
imports. This is similar to how the Thaba-Tseka development project in Lesotho manufactured a need
for Canadian farming equipment (Ferguson, 1994). As for dependency on foreign capital: when
Chinese capital flows in through investments, local businessmen and the government become
complacent and rely too much on Chinese capital for expensive energy infrastructure, creating new
dependencies. Additionally, foreign capital appreciates currencies and interest rates. These crowd out
domestic and public investment (Moyo, 2009), engendering even more dependence on Chinese capital.
In the long run, dependence on foreign capital creates instability risks from capital flight, like in
Indonesia during the Asian Financial Crisis. Dependency thus stunts Africa’s growth and creates risks.

# Weighing the costs and benefits: Long-term risks outweigh short-term benefits

In failing to address and even exacerbating the structural flaws in African economies, Chinese
investments in African energy infrastructure create serious limiting risks that could harm African
economies in the future. The African economic pie is garnished, but none of the missing ingredients
are added, and thus the “development” pie is not fully baked in the long run. I believe genuine African
development needs to address these issues to ensure the sustained prosperity of Africans: the short-
term mirage of economic progress should not be taken as a signal of long-term development.

Is there a way to mitigate long-term harms and amplify the short-term benefits of Chinese
energy investment? There are two potential ways for African nations to ensure that Chinese energy
investments can yield long-term benefits. First, African nations can engage Chinese corporations
through joint ventures with technology transfers. Such policies enable African nations to go beyond
supplying low-value raw materials and capture more value in the green energy value chain. Sengupta
argues that such policies could drive up costs – this might be true in the short-run, but with abundant
raw materials and low labor costs in Africa, costs can eventually come down. These would be similar
to the tech transfer initiatives that Mulumba (2025) proposes for pharmaceuticals. Second, African
nations can make such energy investments public-private partnerships instead, ensuring that the
government and taxpayer revenue / green bonds are actively involved in building energy infrastructure.
This prevents Chinese energy investments from corroding the social contract.
