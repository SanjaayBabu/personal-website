# Output
REMAINED THE SAME
My argument for output has one piece of good news and one piece of bad news; I would argue that output remained roughly the same because the good news outweighs the bad news, a similar picture to what we have observed in past weeks. 

- Bad news first: September Housing Starts had its third consecutive month of declines; declined 5.2% month over month against August's reading. Residential fixed investment (that is, completed homes), is in the formula for investment in GDP; lower housing starts today means that in the future, we can expect residential fixed investment to fall, thus decreasing the investment component of GDP. In line with this logic, having seen the housing starts data, the Atlanta Fed reduced its GDPNow reading from 3.57% to 3.5%. The underlying reason for weak housing starts data is likely (a) concerns that high mortgage rates would make homes less sellable, and (b) increased borrowing costs that developers have to stomach in line with rising rates across the board. Mortgage rates had been rising in the months before September and and have been over 7% since the middle of September. With rising mortgages, more potential home-buyers are priced out of the market, making the sale of homes more difficult. Increasing financing costs for developers also makes construction of homes more difficult. That being said, the decline in housing starts only comprises a small proportion of US output; even the 5.2% month over month decline in housing starts was weighted as a -0.06% decline in the GDPNow model. More crucially, the good news for US output in the last week was observed in consumption, which accounts for a much bigger proportion of US output. 
- Good news: The S&P PMI report reflected broadly a strong picture for output (even if not for prices). New orders growth increased from September's already high reading. Services new businesses jumped to the highest rate since September 2022. The PMI report suggested that such an upturn in orders reflects strong domestic demand and precautionary inventory-building (reflecting some confidence from businesses about future demand). Higher domestic demand & inventory-building factors into higher consumption & investment numbers for GDP. Additionally, the PMI report also showed export goods orders increasing for the first time since June 2024 and the exports services order rising at the fastest rate since July 2023; both of these mean that less exports could be less of a drag on GDP compared to previous months. In aggregate figures, the PMI report had a composite number of 57.6. While this is lower than the number for September by 0.8 points, this month's print is still in strong growth territory. Chris Williamson, the chief business economist for S&P Global, suggested that output prints from the October report are consistent with 5% annualized GDP growth in the start of the fourth quarter, building on 4% signaled growth in Q3. Strong business reports on output suggest potential strong numbers on consumption, investment and potentially net exports, which would outweigh the bad news we had on housing starts this week. While housing might potentially become more of a drag (since starts affect growth with a longer time lag), my sense from this week is that the drag on growth was more limited and is cancelled out by improvements in other areas. 

# Prices
WORSENED
- September PCE will be releasing this coming week; before that print, we can get a sense of the PCE from the Cleveland Fed PCE Nowcast. For September, the forecasted PCE MoM reading is 0.47% headline (annualized 5.79%) and 3.6% YoY. This reflects a worsening in inflation from September's slightly lower YoY headline reading of 3.4%. This worsening likely stems from rising diesel prices; diesel is an important intermediate input that affects all businesses and cannot easily be substituted away from due to its importance in transportation and supply chains. However, the core reading is also up to 0.28% MoM (3.4% annualized) and 3.05% YoY; this reflects price pressures that go beyond just energy and food inputs, reflecting continued pass-through of price increases into consumers. 
- This idea of price pass-through is also observed in the S&P PMI report. There was a marked upturn in output prices across both goods & services, with selling prices increasing in September at a pace comparable to the middle of 2022 (where inflation was high in the post-Covid recovery phase). According to businesses surveyed for the PMI, much of this pass-through in output prices is mechanically due to input price rises, not just for energy (which is a shock that could ostensibly end, though one might argue that energy prices might remain high due to greater uncertainty) but also for other raw materials including steel, aluminum and copper. The S&P PMI reading on output prices prices seems to be a leading indicator for consumer prices (according to the graph on Page 2 of the report), and so we can say that prices are worsening today even if the extent of worsening won't be seen in September PCE data. 

# Labour
IMPROVED
* Initial and continuing claims numbers remained low. Initial claims for the week ending Oct 17 reached their lowest levels since mid July, which was in itself a historic low. The low number of initial claims is a result of low layoffs due to robust corporate profits and strong domestic demand. While the continuing claims number for the week ending Oct 10 went up by 25 thousand, this is statistically a small pickup on a base of 1.7 million and is, in any case, much lower than the 3 year or even 1 year average. Seen together, these are a sign that unemployment is low and likely not worsening given a low-layoff environment. We can thus expect the unemployment rate, the most important metric for the labour market, to remain low & close to maximum employment. 
* More businesses also seem to want to hire new workers, according to the S&P Global Flash US PMI report. Increased backlogs seemed to signal higher future business growth, bolstering confidence for businesses to hire new workers. The S&P report wrote that employment rose in its fastest rate since June 2022, only barring the payrolls jump in September. This increase was also reportedly broad based, with both manufacturing and services businesses anticipating increased hiring of new employees. Overall, this reflects a strong labour market.
