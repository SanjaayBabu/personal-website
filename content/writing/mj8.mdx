# Output
Improved
* Advance estimates for real GDP growth showed 3.4% annualized output growth in Q3. This is an increase from 2.2% in Q2. The defining story for US output of late has been its strength, bolstered by healthy consumer spending and booming investment due to strong spending on data centers and AI. Being the two largest components of GDP, strength in these two sectors bodes well for overall GDP growth. The GDP data release is coherent with this story. Consumer spending increased 3.8% QoQ SAAR (same as Q2) and business investment increased by 12.7% QoQ SAAR (a big jump from Q2, up 3.7%). We did get a new positive detail to this story; strong investment seems to be broadening beyond just data centers & computer equipment. Bloomberg reported that contributions to growth from other categories of investment over the last two quarters were the strongest in three years after a stretch of weakness in 2025 and early 2026. Overall, then, we are getting signs that an already-healthy economy is now potentially even healthier because it is less dependent on strong growth in just one sector. 
* September Durable Goods orders showed 1.8% MoM growth, a sharp increase from the weaker months from May to August. This was notably higher than the expected 1.5% growth. The headline figure was driven by growth in new aircraft orders. It is true that this headline figure tends to be more volatile because spending on aircraft and military hardware tend to fluctuate more. However, the durable goods figure excluding these volatile categories was about 0.5%, which is relatively robust even if lower than August's exceptionally strong reading. Durable Goods orders feed in to the investment portion of GDP with some time lag; the BEA uses the more reliable durable goods shipments data in their computation for GDP. This figure came in at a strong 1.7%. Strong orders in durable goods reflects confidence in future demand, and when these orders are delivered in the future (either in Q4 data reflecting the state of output today or beyond), they will contribute to US output growth. 

# Labour
Worsened
- My argument for a worse labour market hinges on unemployment being somewhat constant, while wage growth has fallen. On unemployment, Initial and continuing jobless claims numbers, as with previous months, remained low (194K and 1701K respectively). They were also lower than the forecasts, though not by much. The jobless claims number for this week is not a significant change or improvement from past weeks and months. It only adds strength to past diagnoses of the US labour market as being in a slow firing state. All in all, high-profile layoff announcements don't seem to have made much of a dent in jobless claims numbers. This might potentially be because the employees in these high-profile companies are more likely to be hired at other places. 
- While unemployment seems to be under control & remaining the same as last week, wage growth has been steadily worsening. This week we received data from the Employment Cost Index, the Fed's preferred metric for wage growth. Once adjusted for inflation, wages fell 0.4% YoY according to Bloomberg. Real wage growth numbers from Dec 2025 and Mar 2026 showed growth albeit at a decreasing pace; but in June and September 2026, real wage growth was negative. Much of this can be explained by higher inflation deflating workers' real wages. Unemployment rate remains low & close to full employment. That being said, there is a strong case to be made that wage growth, while not modeled in our class as a labour market metric, is as important as the unemployment rate when measuring the health of workers. 

# Prices
Remained the same
- September YoY PCE increased by 3.6%, a continuation of recent trends of inflation being far above the Fed's target of 2% PCE YoY inflation. This is largely due to the supply shock from geopolitical tensions in the Middle East disrupting the supply of gas & distillates (with diesel being of particular importance given its role as an intermediate input), although the strength of demand is also a contributor to inflation according to the San Francisco Fed's attribution analysis. That being said, this number isn't that much worse than data from past months; it remains roughly consistent with data from August. Month over month change in PCE for September was 0.3%, the same as that for August. This reveals that inflation might be bad today, but it isn't worsening. 
- We learnt that one major source of inflation is inflation expectations; a prediction that prices will go up in the future will propel more consumption today, which ends up fulfilling the prophecy. After the FOMC voted to hold rates constant, inflation expectations remained somewhat stable and falling in a controlled way; breakeven inflation rates derived from the TIPS market came in at 2.46% for the 5Y and 2.2% for the 5Y5Y forward, somewhat consistent with the readings for past months. With this, it's hard to make the case that inflation expectations are unanchored & getting out of hand; they are just as anchored as they were in past weeks. We should be concerned if expectations start to anchor themselves at a number higher than 2% (either because businesses expect much higher geopolitical or for other reasons), but it appears that expectations are under control for now.
