I normally write these articles when I have a pre-existing opinion or view, and want to test it with others. But this time around, I'm writing this piece without having any clear pathways forward.

The question I'm thinking about is: where does morality factor in in arguments around our duty towards dealing with climate change? Can we construct a moral duty towards dealing with climate change? 

I think the lowest-hanging argument for a moral duty to deal with climate change stems from utilitarian notions. If we fail to deal with climate change, large amounts of suffering can be expected -- both suffering for future human generations, as well as animal & potentially plant life. To the utilitarian, the sheer amount of disutility generated from such suffering far outweighs any short-term utility we enjoy from our current levels of emissions/environmental damage. 

I'm not entirely sure if I buy this argument, though. I think this argument assumes that the livelihoods of tomorrow carry equal importance to the livelihoods of today -- which is true, but something that doesn't quite sit right with many people. That could either result from the common economic property of present bias (making the argument ineffective at compelling present behavioral change). But also, if we potentially found a way to reduce/remove disutility in the future, does it become morally acceptable to continue with our current environmental damage? If we can theoretically reach the perfect tipping point where utility from present emissions = disutility from future environmental harm, should we stop seeking to protect the environment?

Also, there's a further question of whether this "obligation to future generations of other species" applies to all. Do people who choose to have children have an additional duty to protect the environment, since it is their posterity that stands to gain / lose utility based on our current climate impacts? Surely such a policy seems dystopian. We often see our duty towards future generations as applying not only to OUR future generations, but to future generations of humanity more broadly. I'm reminded of the famous John Donne poem "Any man's death diminishes me/For I am involved in mankind." Going even further, we face the question of -- is our duty to mankind and our duty to animals equivalent? If we found a way to protect mankind and not animals, would that be acceptable? Within a utilitarian framework, how do we navigate a situation in which humans can enjoy positive utility (and consequently putting out emissions / climate damage) while animals are suffering negative utility? Most would prioritise human utility. That is often seen as "realistic," but not many would consider it "morally right." 

So if the utilitarian case doesn't work, then can we construct a deontological duty to protect the climate? I wonder whether Kant's theory of rational duty could work here. Kant's first categorical imperative argues that we should only act on a moral principle ("maxim") if we could rationally have (and accept) that principle as a universalized moral law. Workshopping this in the case of climate chamge, a Kantian case could undermine some climate-damaging moral actions. If you were to universalize a maxim like, "I will consume fossil fuels excessively whenever it suits my personal comfort," the resulting world would eventually face ecological collapse. In a state of total environmental breakdown, the very infrastructure, economy, and "comfort" you sought to preserve would cease to exist. Kant also argues for a more general moral duty to treat never treat people as means and always ends, because their capacity for rationality is worth venerating. Hmm. What's the moral status of uncreated, future rationality? I'm sure there's a wealth of literature on this, but I'm yet to see it. I'll delve deeper into that after finishing this piece.

If the utilitarian and deontological cases don't work, then where do we turn to next? I don't know where to categorize this, but something interesting came to mind. Do we have a duty to our *past* selves to clean up our act? Someone I was speaking to about this recently said "Humans have already, in a short span of time, caused irrepairable damage to Earth. Is this how we want to be remembered? But also, after everything is extinct, who will be there to remember humans?" I think there's something interesting here about the moral status of "memory" and wanted to be remembered in a certain way. Perhaps that is tied to our duty to all the generations of humans that *didn't* destroy earth. By earning a reputation as an earth-destroying species, we are unwantedly tarnishing their reputation. I'm not sure if arguments on reputation are morally sound, though. It could be, but I'd have to see a better case for why I can treat an action as morally better just because it cleans up the way I'm perceived by others. 

Climate change advocacy often takes on a deeply moral tone. But I'm struggling to see how, from a moral philsophy perspective, we can assemble a robust moral case to deal with climate change. I'm not saying this to be abrasive and argue that a moral case doesn't exist, but rather I'm looking at this from the perspective of curiosity.
