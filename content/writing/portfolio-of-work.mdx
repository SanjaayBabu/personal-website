I’ve been reflecting on the big ways in which my thinking has changed since I started my university studies. One of the big things I feel I have been (and want to continue) focussing on is building a portfolio of work. 

First, defining key terms. Both options are attempts to signal competence — to current and future employers, with the view of attaining promotions or building your dream career niche. But they look different and have different externalities. 

Resume-building focusses on single-line items for whom context speaks for itself; an impressive resume generally banks on the reader making associations between what they see and what they assume. “CEO” = competent. “Oxbridge” = intelligent. The signal also looks like, as the name suggests, a resume; often, a one-page document that demands people to fuss over formatting, using the right action words, and choosing the right activities. Even when output metrics are stated, they’re focussed on being “impressive” — 100x improvement on growth (what was the base?), wrote 5 white papers (on what?). 

Building a portfolio of work, on the other hand, focusses on output. The work needs to speak for itself. For example, don’t just say you worked on development; put out your white paper, your main ideas, and let people decide for themselves whether that was good work, work that signals quality. This signal looks like, perhaps, a personal portfolio website, or a GitHub link, etc. (it really depends on the field). Descriptions are longer-form, and tell the stories of why something was made, for whom, and what limitations there are. 

Why are portfolios of work better than resumes? Why is building a portfolio of work a better strategy than building a resume? 

First: Resumes tell a story, but it’s ultimately a facade. In my view, resumes are a massive signalling failure (so are behavioral interviews). Where there are resumes, there’s a lemon market — the people who do best are those who can tell a better story about themselves, rather than necessarily do the work best. On the other hand, portfolios *are* the story. It’s hard to cover for sloppy work. Work quality is the foremost consideration. 

Consider two individuals — one who used their summer interning at a big-name bank / firm but did very little work there apart from pushing papers; and another who took the summer to build something themselves. Who learnt more? Who’s more experienced in the world of business? Probably the latter. But as far as a resume-centered job market is concerned, the former is the one who succeeds. 

Second: I think resumes create adverse incentives for students; you’re focussed on **getting the position** rather than being prepared to **thrive in the position**. Of course, you could always get good once you’re in the position; getting it is just the first step. But what if we all re-thought this? What if the focus was about being prepared to assume that position? Having the disposition, experience and judgment to do well in that position? The “rules of the game” (which are resume-centered in most industries right now) dictate players’ behavior. The behavior now is strategic, focussed on “landing the job” rather than doing good work every step of the way. 

On the other hand, a world in which quality is signalled through demonstrated high-quality work (doesn’t that sound commonsensical!) could potentially produce much better work. It’s also probably much more meritocratic — you can’t just hide behind your flashy degree or your internship with your father’s friend’s office, but rather need to prove your worth doing good work. It probably creates a less high-pressure environment: if *good work* is the success metric, and there are many different ways to do good work — some might be videos, some might be essays, some might be photographs — then there are many different ways to succeed. You can’t really “get ahead” of people, and if anything, the better strategy is to bounce ideas on your different projects. 

Finally: A portfolio-building conception of work attaches value to the small things people want to do and are good at, but might not fit in a resume. It creates a space for productive *output* that doesn’t fit in their day job. It helps the civil servant who joined the service because they cared about a specific policy area to go on side quests, and still get “rewarded” for having a pet area and exploring it. It reveals the true value of that intern who’s being asked to take notes, but is in fact a highly talented and passionate artist and could totally makeover the firm’s website.

I know the response many will have — resumes have their value, because who has time to review an entire portfolio of work? This might be true, but I guess that reviewing portfolios of work could become a very high ROI activity, as the kind of work that often goes in resumes gets easily automated. It’s hard to suss out lateral thinking, creativity and interdisciplinarity from resumes; it’s easier on websites. 

Could portfolios of work also go down a race to the bottom and become as trite as resumes have become? Maybe. But I think it can’t get triter. Ultimately, the work has to speak for itself in a portfolio of work, so I guess there’s only so bad it can get. Good work will still have a louder voice.
