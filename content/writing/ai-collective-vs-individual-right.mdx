Like many of my peers, I was struck by a claim made by a Singaporean parliamentarian that access to premium artificial intelligence is a right — a right akin to that of having food, and shelter. The language of rights comes with responsibilities, so the corollary responsibility that follows from AI being a right is this: governments have a responsibility to provide free AI to their citizens, for the public to use. 

I can’t speak to the parliamentarian’s logic, but the most popular argument to broaden *public* access to AI centers around equitable access to a powerful tool. If there was a tool which has the capacity to supercharge work, learning, and even personal development, then it would be unfair for some, and not others, to have access to it. Even a $20/month subscription can be prohibitively expensive for some. So governments must provide people with a level playing field and universalize access to the most powerful models. 

I think this doesn’t go far enough, though; and worse yet, I think it’s built on a misleading assumption — that the playing field *can* be level. There is already a big rift between the “power” users of AI who are building agentic workflows that automate most of their work, or even independently runs its own businesses, and the average user who uses AI as a powerful Google. And everything in between. In my view, this differential is structural. That gap has always existed, albeit in different forms. People are also compensated differently based on where they lie within this gap. It’s not going to go away just because a government-sponsored login to ChatGPT comes in the mail. 

So if the playing field can’t be level, then how can we go further to preserve some semblance of fairness in our societies? Rather than treating access to AI as an individual right, governments should invest in AI as a public good. 

Ezra Klein sets up a valuable picture of what this looks like in [his essay for the New York Times](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/05/31/opinion/artificial-intelligence-public-good.html):  
- Building useful AI tools that enables people to engage more meaningfully with government and public services, such as filing taxes; 
- Creation of, and better utilization of, datasets that enable better policymaking; 
- Reducing the “access to compute” gap between private sector actors and public good actors (e.g. universities, hospitals, military);
- Tapping on AI to promote innovation, like studying under-researched diseases, or developing new treatments.  
Additionally, I would include that another critical detail is ensuring that small businesses can also tap on AI, so that it’s not just the massive MNCs that we often think of when we think about the “private sector” have access to AI. 

What are the benefits of this approach? First, I think it solves the equity issue better. Rather than the government’s responsibility stopping at providing individuals with access to AI models, governments are now held to a higher standard — ensuring that people can reap several crucial *benefits* of AI, regardless of how sound they are at using it personally. Second, I think this approach can rebuild trust in AI — seeing ways in which AI can actually achieve sustained, broad “good” ensures that people feel broadly benefited by AI innovation

But most critically, I think government should do this because they are probably uniquely suited to doing it. Many public goods are often “non-excludable” and “non-rivalrous”. I’m guessing it’ll be particularly so in the AI age; I’m not very sure if intellectual property rules, for example in the case of medical innovations, can keep up in the age of AI, given how different groups can independently arrive at similar results. And when goods are neither excludable nor rivalrous, profits might not go far enough as incentives to shape private sector behaviour. 

I’m not claiming that the two policies — broadening access to AI, and treating AI as a public good — are mutually exclusive. (Although I think that claim can be made out; consider the opportunity cost of providing universal access to AI when some are just not going to use it; it’s also a completely untargeted policy.) Just that the “right of access to AI” framework can create serious shortfalls in genuinely setting citizens up for AI success.
