# OUTPUT
- Retail Sales are up 1.2% Month-over-Month. Calculated in nominal terms, the retail sales figure calculates how much consumers spent this month on finished goods & services for personal and household use rather than resale. Increased retail sales suggests that consumption expenditure remains strong, reassuring worries from the weak Michigan consumer sentiment report from the previous week. High consumption expenditure suggests that output is higher; the Wall Street Journal reported that sales in the categories that feed into the formula for gross domestic product rose by about 1.4%. Granted, the fact that this figure is measured in nominal terms is a limitation. Another limitation of this figure is the fact the base (the month of July) was a month with weaker spending, given that Amazon Prime Day was moved to June. However, I still read this number positively because (a) the 1.2% figure also exceeded forecasters' expectations of 0.8%, which would already have accounted for the lower July sales, and (b) sales from non-gasoline retails such as electronics, appliance stores and "non-store retailers" including e-commerce were also up by 2.6%, showing that increased retail sales were broad based and not just explained by higher prices paid for gasoline. 
- The FOMC, in its Summary of Economic Projections for September, forecasted 2.3% GDP growth for 2026; this is an upward revision from their projection of 2.2% in July. The FOMC governors' strengthened prediction for output signals that output is strengthening; this is consistent with my other data point mentioned above with regards to retail sales this week. 

# LABOUR
- Initial Jobless Claims for the week through September 12 were down to 196k. This was a drop of 10k from the previous, unrevised reading of 206k. This was well under market expectations of 208k as well. This should be read in tandem with the Continuing Jobless Claims number for the week through September 5, of 1.73mn and also down by 39k from the week before. These numbers are very low from a historical point of view, and are a good indicator of a strong labour market with low unemployment. The initial claims are roughly the same as that number from May 2026, which was in itself a 60 year low. This suggests that the labour market is strong & consistent with full employment, a claim that the Federal Open Market Committee backed. While we saw no unemployment figures this week, the low initial & continuing jobless claims numbers suggests that the labour market (primarily measured through unemployment) is no worse, and somewhat better than the previous week. 
- In addition to fewer jobless claims, ADP Weekly Employment Change numbers revealed that US private employers added an average of 16,250 jobs per week in the four weeks ending August 29, 2026. This is an increase from the preceding four weeks, where around 12k jobs were added per week. While this raw number is not very high, the fact that new private sector hiring remains stable amidst an already tight labour market is a good sign for the labour market. 

# PRICES
- The American Automobile Association (AAA) reported that gas prices increased to $4.47 a gallon on Friday, 18 September, up from $4.30 on Friday the week prior. Diesel, a critical factor to US supply chains given their use by transport trucks, hit a new all-time high of $6.45 a gallon. This is partly in response to a potential new supply shock emerging over the last two weeks with Houthi rebels' attack on the Saudi East-West pipeline and their seizure of an island in the Bab-el-Mandeb Strait, yet another chokepoint in the global oil trade. One factor mitigating this increase in gas prices is the small fall in futures pricing for November deliveries of oil. While this might portend potential easing of the tight energy market, there is no such evidence of that from this week's AAA numbers.
- One interesting development in prices after the Fed's decision to hike this week was in inflation expectations. Market-derived inflation expectations (Nominal Treasury Yield - TIPS Yield) saw the 5Y breakeven rate come down significantly to 2.31%, the 10Y at 2.33% and the 5Y5Y forward rate (5Y breakeven inflation in 5 Years) at 2.35%. In the last few months, I've rarely seen the 5Y5Y forward rate be higher than the 5Y breakeven rate, likely because there seemed to be a consensus that the high present inflation was more transitory. While this is a single data point and we will need to see market data from this week to see whether this is a trend, a continuation of this trend might suggest that the market is seeing a future with more sustained inflation beyond simply oil-shock related inflation. Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis President Neel Kashkari also said this week that inflation is in all aspects of the economy, including in services; the Supercore services PCE data from July supports this claim. Sustained high inflation expectations undermine the Fed's move to hike rates in order to control inflation; and given that the Fed's move to control inflation was based on the seemingly structural sources of inflation, the fact that inflation expectations are high in the longer term is a cause for concern.
