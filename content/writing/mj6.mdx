# Output
IMPROVED
- Retail sales expanded 0.8% in September; expansion in retail sales generally manifests itself in GDP as an increase in consumption. This was higher than the expected 0.4% average from a forecaster poll that the Wall Street Journal conducted. One caveat is that the retail sales number is nominal, meaning that the total dollar amount spent on retail goods could have increased purely because of increases in prices. Yet, the MoM expansion in retail sales is higher than the MoM headline inflation print; using a Fisher approximation to make the nominal retail sales number real (0.2%), there is still positive growth over a base that was already high from a strong showing in August. 
- Industrial production grew 0.7% MoM in September, the highest number in five months and far exceeding expectations of 0.3%. High industrial production can manifest as increased output because industrial goods feed into the Consumption and Investment portions of GDP. The high industrial production figure can be explained by broad based growth across manufacturing, utilities, and mining. 

# Prices
WORSENED
- CPI YoY Headline Inflation print was at 3.7%, 30bp higher than the YoY for Aug, and even surpassing forecaster expectations of 3.6%. Concerningly, the MoM headline inflation print was 0.6%, and when one annualizes this, it is a figure of 7.44%. Data from the BLS shows that almost half of the overall CPI increase was a result of the increase in gasoline prices as a result of refinery constraints & disruptions of oil supply in the Middle East due to conflict in Iran (US-Iran) and Saudi Arabia (against the Houthis). It is thus unsurprising that the core inflation print (which excludes food and energy) was much lower at 2.6% YoY and 0.3% MoM (3.67% annualized). While these numbers are comparatively lower than headline figures, I still read this as a worsening of inflation. First, underlying inflation being at 3.67% annualized is elevated, even if not unprecedented. This month continues a trend of MoM inflation being persistently high. Secondly, the fact that it was above forecaster expectations across indicators is concerning; the fact that inflation was worse than expected could in fact cause higher future inflation. Businesses might build elevated expectations into their prices. Thirdly, the fact that transportation was the category which showed the highest growth in MoM Core CPI growth reveals that the price increases from the oil shock is being passed on to consumers; given that transportation is important in most sectors of the economy and one can't easily substitute between goods when transportation increases costs, we can estimate current PCE levels to also be high. 
- This last point is also reinforced by the reading for the Cleveland Fed Inflation Nowcast. The October 16 estimate for Core PCE YoY inflation, the Fed's measure of choice, is 3.13%. This figure is at around 3.4% when annualizing the Core PCE MoM figure instead. This is far above the 2% inflation target set by the Fed for price stability, and is similar to the August PCE reading which persuaded Fed governors to hike rates by 25bp in September. 

# Labour
IMPROVED
- Initial Jobless Claims print at 199k for the week ending October 10, marginally lower than the forecaster consensus of 200k. While this is very marginally higher than the previous week's figure of 198k, this figure in absolute terms is historically low, showing that not many workers are being freshly laid off; this can thus be considered a continuation of a "slow fire" labor market. Continuing Jobless Claims print was at 1710k for the week ending October 3, matching the forecasters' consensus. This figure is also stable and historically low. One could view this figure skeptically; the stability of the continuing jobless claims number shows that previously unemployed workers might be remaining unemployed, revealing some kind of structural unemployment stemming from the misfit between the kinds of skills demanded and supplied. Yet, this is undermined by the fact that this number is low by historical standards. FRED data (updated in the first week of October) on people unemployed for 27 weeks and over, a good indicator of structural unemployment, picked up in the middle of 2025 but has since been stable.  Thus, I wouldn't be too concerned that structural unemployment is rampant & corroding labour market health.
- On the Philly Fed MBOS survey, a supermajority of respondents in manufacturing businesses either indicated no change or increases in hiring; in a labour market already characterized by low unemployment, this is a good sign of labour market health. No respondents to the MBOS survey expected a declining in number of employees in the coming six months, though this is perhaps less informative given respondents tend to underestimate labour market layoffs as we previously discussed. More critically, while a larger proportion of respondents expected no change in the coming 6 months compared to the same number in September, there is still about a third of respondents who expected new hiring in the coming two quarters; again, either an increase or no change in hiring is good news in an already tight labour market.
