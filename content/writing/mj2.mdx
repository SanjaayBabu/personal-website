# Output

I would argue output IMPROVED this week.

The S&P Global Purchasing Managers Index Flash figures showed strong growth in US business activity across both manufacturing and services. Both indices beat consensus: manufacturing at 57 (vs. 53.5 expected) and services at 58.7 (vs. 55.7). The composite number of 58.4 is the highest figure since July 2021 (the post-Covid recovery boom), indicating broad-based growth in business activity. The report showed severe supply chain challenges (in terms of delivery times and input costs), but also reported the fastest expansion in new orders — a critical indicator of demand. Bloomberg concluded that "the latest data from S&P Global suggest that strong demand is outweighing the jump in energy costs and supply chain snarls brought on by the Iran war." I think this report is a mixed picture for the economy broadly, because it does show that inflation is worsening. But as far as output is concerned, the numbers seen here is good news. Broad-based strength in business activity means that manufacturing & services output is likely getting stronger, feeding into the Consumption part of the GDP formula. 

The Non Defense Capital Goods Orders Excluding Aircraft number, showing how much capital expenditure businesses are making, increased 1.6% month-on-month, significantly outperforming last month (0.6%) and consensus (0.5%). This continues trends of increasing capital investment this year, particularly for primary metals, machinery, computers and electrical equipment. Core capital goods shipments (rather than orders) excluding the volatile components of aircraft and defense spending grew 0.6% month over month continuing strong growth in the prior two months. Shipments can't be cancelled, so the 0.6% and broader trend of strong capital good shipment is economically significant. Overall, these suggest that the Investment component of the GDP formula is doing well; Bloomberg concluded that Friday's report by the Census bureau "set the stage for robust third-quarter economic growth."

# Labour

I would argue the labour market IMPROVED this week.

Initial Jobless Claims numbers fell even further last week. Initial claims fell by 1000 to 197k, even though economists surveyed by WSJ expected this number to rise to 201k. Continuing claims numbers jumped by 2k (a very small aberration), and this number is still much lower compared to historical baselines. As I mentioned last week, these numbers are historically low, with the initial numbers this week being comparable to the May 2026 numbers which were themselves 60 year lows. These low jobless claim figures are evidence that layoffs are low in the current labour market, strengthening the case for more decisive action on inflation.

The S&P Global PMI Flash report for September showed large increases in backlogs of uncompleted orders (highest since May 2022) which "encouraged firms to take on more staff." Employment grew at a rate not seen since September 2022. These increases are broad based across both services and manufacturing. These numbers suggest that hiring of new workers is increasing. I noticed that these numbers told a different story from what we saw in the Philly Fed Manufacturing Business Outlook Survey (MBOS), which implied that business, uncertain about future outlook, are lengthening employee work weeks rather than hiring new workers. A potential reason for the difference could be methodological; perhaps the S&P survey's national coverage shows us some information that the Philly Fed specific survey might not; perhaps Philly firms are less certain about whether increased new orders demand will last, while in other parts of the country, firms are more confident about strong demand in the future.

# Prices

I would argue that prices DETERIORATED this week.

The Michigan Survey of Consumers showed that consumers expect prices to go up 3.4% annually over the coming 5 year period; this is a 10bp increase compared to the same number from last month. Being a survey of the "common American," Michigan inflation expectations number might not work well as a wholly objective indicator that prices are going to get worse. This number is also significantly higher than the 5Y breakeven inflation expectations comparing Nominal and TIPS yields (at 2.34%). That being said, these limitations might give it even more credence; the Wall Street Journal reported that "higher expected inflation could change consumers’ behavior." One possible mechanism by which this might happen is: consumers, expecting prices to go up in the future, might buy more now & demand more wage growth. This would in turn increase demand- and wage-driven inflation. Consumer inflation expectation could thus become a self-fulfilling prophecy.

Diesel prices continue their run up, with Monday (Sept 21) price at $6.529 per gallon (around 4 percent higher than last week). Diesel (unlike gasoline) is not exactly just a "final good & service" that consumers can buy and substitute around; it's a critical intermediate input for trucks that transport final goods or raw materials around the US. Continually rising diesel prices could be passed on to consumers in the form of increased final prices, worsening inflation not just in headline numbers but also core. Apollo characterized this as a case of "pass-through the Fed cannot dismiss as transitory." Notably, President Trump is considering an export ban for diesel to keep more of the refined output within the US; economists writing for the New York Times argued that this might bring down diesel costs in the short run, but that in the long run, refineries would simply cut production, in turn keeping costs high. With that in mind, I don't expect sustainable relief in diesel prices until the root cause — the war in Iran — is addressed. Pass-through is likely.
