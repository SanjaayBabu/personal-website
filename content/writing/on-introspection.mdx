For a summer class, I read Park & Millora (2012) on the relevance of reflection. I include an AI summary below, and a personal comment afterward.

# AI summary of Park & Millora

This study empirically investigates three central research questions using nationwide longitudinal survey data from first-year and third-year college students: how reflection patterns vary across demographic traits and academic majors; whether student self-ratings in specific developmental outcomes vary by reflection levels; and if self-reflection or reflective writing significantly impacts a student's Ethic of Caring, Leadership, and Psychological Well-Being (PWB) by their third year. Grounded in constructivist development theories, the authors hypothesized that intentional reflection helps students process shifting identities and social responsibilities, ultimately reinforcing positive developmental benchmarks.

The headline conclusion is a striking dual reality: while reflective practices are powerful catalysts for social and cognitive development, they simultaneously pose risks to immediate emotional comfort. The regression analyses revealed that both individual self-reflection and faculty-assigned reflective writing are among the strongest positive predictors of an increased Ethic of Caring and enhanced Leadership skills. However, personal self-reflection was significantly negatively related to Psychological Well-Being. The authors conclude that as students deconstruct simplistic worldviews and wrestle with complex societal inequities or personal growth, reflection naturally induces anxiety and stress. Rather than avoiding reflection, the paper concludes with a strong recommendation that higher education institutions must build deliberate support networks to help students safely navigate and reconstruct these complex meaning-making systems.

# My Thoughts

Reading this paper made me think of a really interesting debate I've been seeing re-emerging today about whether reflection and introspection are actually good for you. The re-emerging debate started with Marc Andreessen, a venture capitalist and former software engineer, saying in an interviewLinks to an external site. that he practices 'zero introspection' and that great people in the past were more focused on doing things rather than dwelling in the past.

The common response from most of my college / high school friends who went on to higher education was quite expected -- people were shocked, and pushed back. We've been told that introspection is good; the ethics of Marcus Aurelius & stoicism has permeated our lives, and so it's become a norm for many to see journalling and reflection as part of living an aesthetically "good" life -- lumped together with other well-being practices such as meditation, yoga, exercise, volunteering, and others. 

Park and Millora uses data to show that the truth lies somewhere in between. Yes, reflection is good for you. But so is doing things and getting involved in experiences.  Leadership trainings, collegiate sports, co-curriculars, maybe even travel, all give us meaningful experiences. Such experiences, complemented with reflection, is what results in character-building and leadership progress. Experiences with no reflection makes for a life that just flies past you. But reflection with no experiences, in my view, is what brings you down the kinds of spirals that, as Park and Millora point out, could worsen your psychological well-being. 

I think the most important takeaway is to have some awareness of how centered your life is at any given point. Insofar as some reflection is necessary for this, I think Andreessen is wrong. Perhaps his opposition is more to how reflection has permeated into pop-culture and has become a performance, instead of being a genuinely transformative experience.
