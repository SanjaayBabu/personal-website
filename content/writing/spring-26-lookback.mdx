# Climate change: A wicked problem
I knew of how severe a threat climate change was, but this class gave me a clear conceptual framework to make sense of what climate change really is — rising sea levels, effects on the cryosphere, extreme weather events, the role of fossil fuels and human carbon forcings, etc. It's very clear that human actions are causing the climate changes we are seeing, and it's very clear that our current pace of climate damage is thoroughly unsustainable. This isn't going to end well for us.

What can we do about it? That's the big question I'm left with at the end of this course. The course presented the science, but we'll need more than just climate science to tackle this problem, in my view. 

Climate change, as a policy issue, taps into some of the biggest dilemmas humanity has had to deal with. Some of the include: 
- **Whether individual agency matters more, or whether we are always at the behest of structures & institutions** - should individuals be expected to buy carbon credits to offset their travels, consume less plastic, etc. when corporations make up the bulk of emissions?
- **Past vs present** — are developing countries justified in [their argument](https://www.thenationalnews.com/business/energy/africa-wants-the-same-opportunity-as-the-west-had-to-develop-1.728836) that "we are morally sound in using coal (the dirtiest fossil fuels) because the Western powers had their fair share"? If so, then what obligations do Western countries have if they care about climate change?
- **What we owe the future** - what matters more: the standard of living today, which is a real problem, or the standard of living tomorrow? Do we have an equal obligation to the humans of tomorrow as that of the humans today? 

I think there's also the question of how to construct a moral case for dealing with climate change. In response to the article I wrote previously on [ethical messaging for climate advocacy](https://www.sanjaaybabu.com/writing/ethics-of-climate-advocacy), I heard some interesting arguments. 
* One was that the **utilitarian framework still applies**, because at some point, it's going to become a higher-utility decision to live a greener life. The cost of living a green live will also fall as technology develops. 
* Another interesting argument I heard was that our obligation to the Earth is weirdly **contractual** - our chance of living on this Earth (and benefiting from its fruits) is so miniscule and unprobabilistic that we have an obligation to give back. Contractualist views of moral obligation risk being flimsy, though, because they rely on mutual consent and a meeting of minds. We didn't "choose" to be born, so this argument doesn't appeal to a whole bunch of people.

Overall, my climate change class has compelled me to think further about this wicked problem. We do have to act, despite all the obstacles that stand in our way. This means designing new systems, a new message and a new morality. I'll be looking into this further.

# Development in Africa: Going it alone

Going into this class, I was broadly a supporter of development aid. In fact, in a dinner conversation with a friend, I talked about how I would like to see Singapore take on a more prominent role in disbursing development aid.

This class showed how aid often looks better than it actually is. [Dead Aid](https://www.amazon.com/Dead-Aid-Working-Better-Africa/dp/0374532125), a book by Dambisa Moyo, presents compelling arguments for how aid fosters corruption, creates dependencies, and harms domestic production by promoting imports. Aid is often offered with a certain conception of what "development" means and looks like (what Foucauldians call a dominant "discourse"); it's often also wrought by confirmation bias, with development agencies "diagnosing" the problems they think are important, even though there are far bigger challenges.

The most compelling argument I heard against development aid comes from Ferguson's piece on how development is an [Anti-Politics Machine](https://s3.amazonaws.com/files.commons.gc.cuny.edu/wp-content/blogs.dir/1989/files/2014/08/Ferguson-The-Anti-Politics-Machine.pdf). Analyzing the Thaba-Tseka project in Lesotho, Ferguson told the story of how the developmentalists from the Canadian Development Bank and the World Bank force-fitted their prescriptions of what a “developed” society looks like on the institutions of Lesotho, and this ended up causing more harm than good. Pre-existing institutions became no better, only worse. 

If development aid from other countries potentially does more harm than good, then what’s the way forward for African nations that do indeed need development? This class made me see how, perhaps, the best way forward is for them to go it alone. 

African countries often have what it takes to develop themselves. A wealth of natural resources, a young population that is looking for jobs, and countries within the continent that are looking for new places to invest. Using these assets, they need to find a way to boost production and manufacturing. This might require a few years of pain; there might be a need to engage in protectionist policies to ensure that import-substitution succeeds, and this is sure to be met with retaliation by European Union trade partners. But this feels like necessary pain for African nations to capture more value in the value chain, rather than sticking to a consumption-based economy. 

I think one big asset that African nations should seek to tap on is regional partnerships. There should be more mechanisms for richer African nations to underwrite bond issuances for poorer African nations, and for wealthy African conglomerates to regionalize. Some of this requires Africa to resolve the conflicts that have persisted since the colonial powers first set foot in Africa. Perhaps the winds of such change have come, with African nations realizing that neither the West nor the Global South can carry the burden of African development. This needs to be done themselves.

Perhaps this African-led development is not only the most achievable path to development, but also the most dignified one. Africa can no longer be seen by others as an infantilized continent in need of helping if they refuse external aid; Africans too rebuild a sense of agency in their development and pride in their growth. 

As an intern in the Starling Institute, this is a point I hope to explore further — how best can multilateral institutions help Africa engage in this self-driven growth, rather than depending on others? 

# Calculus & Econometrics 
I’m lumping the two of these together because frankly, there isn’t much narrative reflection I can have on these two subjects. So perhaps this is where the “academic reflection” bit  crosses the line into personal reflection.

I honestly really enjoyed these two classes, far more than I would have initially expected. I think that’s because I always had the mental model that I didn’t have a “math brain,” that it wasn’t my forte. And perhaps that might be true — perhaps it is true that mathematics doesn’t come as naturally to me as other fields like the social sciences. I was pleasantly surprised, though, at how much I connected with these fields. I enjoyed the systematic thinking and problem-solving opportunities these classes gave me, and because I was no longer comparing myself against those who I thought had a “math brain,” I was actually able to enjoy the process of studying it. 

I definitely see myself taking more advanced maths and econometrics classes! Probably not with the view of majoring / minoring in maths, but just enjoying the process. Who cares if you have a “math brain?” Do what you like doing, is what I’ve learnt. 

# European Union: Future or fading?
This class was another highlight of my semester. A house course, it was taught by a sophomore at Duke who has a uniquely strong understanding of the European Union having interned with its institutions and connected with the politicians who are instrumental in its running. 

I learnt about the structure and processes of the EU, as well as its approach towards defence, sustainability, tech and other key policy areas. Throughout the class, a big question we were asking was whether the EU’s model and approach are ready for the future, or would it eventually diminish in importance over time? 

I can see the EU probably maintaining its relevance going forward. There have been good faith attempts to adapt the EU to the future it anticipates — for example, they have been relatively united in their effort to strengthen defence cooperation, and they have recognized the need for a leaner framework in achieving sovereign AI capabilities. 

Sure, there are challenges. The EU is still quite divided along wealth lines. Bureaucracy continues to be a big challenge. But still, the EU has had some very important successes that have faded into the background, like its robust common market and its success at regulating data privacy. Much like many other institutions, we often only see its failures.

Will the EU be a superpower in itself? I highly doubt so. Is that the metric of a successful multilateral institution? Probably not. In pondering whether the EU has been a success story, I think the more important question we often neglect is what our metrics in evaluating a multilateral institution is. Multilateralism isn’t, and shouldn’t be expected to be, a silver bullet solution to every challenge. It should be employed as a tool whenever economies of scale are helpful, but that’s all. So as long as the EU can act effectively in aligning its militaries, in tapping on cross-border collaborations to build AI infrastructures, etc., one shouldn’t expect any more from the EU.
