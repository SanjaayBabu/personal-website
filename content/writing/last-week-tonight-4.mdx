## Developing Africa’s pharmaceutical potential

This weekend, we spoke with [<u>Karrar Karrar</u>](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/team/k/karrar-karrar), a current PhD student in the University of Manchester, about how Africa can build a domestic pharmaceutical industry. 

The state of medicine in Africa is highly challenging. African countries suffer an unfortunate “double disease burden” — essentially, Africa is seeing the proliferation of both infectious diseases (e.g. malaria, tuberculosis) and non-infectious diseases (e.g. hypertension, obesity). There is therefore high demand for medicine.

However, there is simply not enough spending in healthcare to provide for the demand. While OECD countries spend $4,321 per head per year on healthcare, African nations spend only $83 per head. Even when adjusting for currency differences, this isn’t a lot of money! And it’s certainly a lot lower than the 15% they promised to spend under the Abuja convention. This is a typical case of the Pareto problem (using rough numbers to illustrate, Africa suffers 80% of the disease burden but spends only 20% of global healthcare spending, while the inverse is true in the OECD countries). 

Is healthcare finance the only issue? Not quite. There are several other issues:

1. *Lack of manufacturing capacity & import dependency* — this leaves Africa susceptible to withholding of medicines, such as what we saw during the COVID-19 pandemic. “Vaccine nationalism” was a phenomenon where countries protected themselves first (as one would expect), only providing medicines/vaccines far later once the domestic health crisis was almost fully resolved. COVID wasn’t the first case of this happening — the same happened during the H1N1 and HIV/AIDS crises. 

2. *Poor regulatory mechanisms* — paves the way for massive spread of fakes. Due to low trust in domestically-obtained medicines, Africans end up intentionally spending more on imported medicine compared to generics produced in Africa. Spending is already low; the lack of trust adds additional costs. Also, low trust affects medicine supply: Why would Pfizer invest in building manufacturing capacity in Africa if they aren’t going to sell anything, because the market is overrun with toxic counterfeits pretending to be authentic and selling at lower prices?

What can we do? Mr. Karrar felt that there were three key gaps that had to be resolved: material, regulatory, and epistemic. To resolve regulatory gaps, Africa has created one regulation industry. To bring back knowledge on drug manufacturing, Africa has sought to collaborate with pharmaceutical manufacturers from the Global South, such as China and India. 

Still, big issues remain. Healthcare spending is unlikely to rise in the short-to-medium term, because the tax base in Africa remains tiny. Much economic activity in Africa remains informal, and thus capturing taxes remains hard. Most tax revenue is obtained through resource sales, and not from citizens — there is therefore no sense that “healthcare is a right that the government owes the citizens”. Thus. there is little societal pressure to build better healthcare systems. 

## The qualitative turn in research?

I had the chance to attend a wonderful seminar led by Dr. Shikhar Singh, a professor of political science here at Duke, about research methods. We learnt about the various ways that researchers draw conclusions from what we know about the world. 

The most memorable learning point I had from this sharing was the notion that research papers are essentially meant to explore a “puzzle” — why does Y happen despite X? He provided a great example — Why is air pollution not a big issue in Indian elections, even though it’s such a palpable reality in many Indian cities? These make for compelling studies. Not all studies establish causal links; rather, “descriptive” studies focus on describing something about the world as it is.

At the end of the sharing, I had a big question for Dr. Singh. How does the academy feel about some newer computational linguistics techniques that are becoming increasingly popular amongst data journalists? Dr. Singh talked about the massive potential for AI in research. In one case, Professors Graham Straus and Andy Hall wanted to understand whether AI could replicate the human research process. They gave Claude Code a detailed set of instructions, and let it conduct the study independently with little supervision. The resulting paper it generated was comparable in quality and results to their own study, and they argued that it made the same kinds of mistakes that a human would make — ostensibly, ‘forgivable’ errors. The full paper is [<u>here</u>](https://www.andrewbenjaminhall.com/Straus_Hall_Claude_Audit.pdf), and the abstract is worth a glance-through. 

This made me wonder — what, really, is the delta of human researchers? Perhaps the key was in the detailed set of instructions. Having a feel for what open questions are worth exploring, and the creativity/imagination to conceive of new puzzles to explore, might be the core of what makes us valuable in an academic environment. Also, prompting is a skill. I wish we spent more time in school learning how to use AI tools the way Straus and Hall did, to guide our research. 

The question of how schools can best educate their students on AI is still an big, open one. I do hope, however, that we reach a working consensus soon. Currently, I feel that exploring and figuring out a good “working relationship” with AI tools based on my own strengths is peripheral to my college education — something to explore “on the side”. But we might have to put this front and center soon.

Perhaps colleges need to be more agile with this. There’s never going to be a perfect answer in how colleges can prepare students to use AI. But they need to start somewhere. They also could get students to come onboard to co-create potential implementations of AI in school. In some cases, students are more “tapped in” to AI developments, and probably have something to add. I’ll be watching this space closely!
