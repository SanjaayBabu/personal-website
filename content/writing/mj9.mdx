# Output
Remained the same

- Wholesale inventories increased more than expected in September, with an increase of 0.7% MoM; wholesale sales increased even more, by 2.8% MoM. All of these are nominal figures, which is an important caveat; rising prices could mean that this increase in wholesale sales is purely due to price increases. However, this figure is still higher than the inflation print, so by a Fisher approximation, I can still conclude that the wholesale sales went up. The increase in wholesale sales is good news for output because this factors into the consumption portion of GDP; strong wholesale sales means that the biggest component of GDP remains robust and growing. The increase in wholesale inventories, assuming that producers don't have it completely wrong, could indicate some optimism about future output; the reason I make this case is that the increase in inventories is somewhat controlled, and its growth doesn't seem to evidence goods being produced but not purchased. While this looks like a strong picture for output, I would say it's not much different from where things have been in past weeks, with inventory growth being somewhat stable when compared to the past month's growth of 0.6%. Most importantly, the factors underlying stable inventory growth — electrical goods & hardware (products of the AI capex buildout) — seem at risk with expectations of increased regulations under the Democrats, so I wouldn't say the stable growth in inventory now is an indication of output necessarily stronger than it has previously been.

- The Atlanta Fed GDPNow for Q4 was updated on Monday, and was revised down slightly from 3.55% to 3.48%. This was driven by a change in private inventories, although this is not contradictory with my argument in the previous paragraph because the GDPNow data release was on Monday and the wholesale inventories data came in afterwards. The decline in the GDPNow numbers is not very substantial, particularly since the error margin on GDPNow Q4 is quite large for now given that Q4 will be informed by much more data that is yet to be released. Regardless, the output numbers are somewhat stable for Q4 in the mid to high 3.4% range, with no major improvements or declines.

# Prices
Worsened

- October CPI print showed sustained high inflation. Headline MoM inflation was 0.4% (4.88% annualized), while core was 0.3% (3.66% annualized). Granted, the headline number was slightly below the September print. That being said, I still read this as a deterioration in prices because this month's CPI print also seems to show clearer evidence of pass-through from diesel into other categories. The detailed CPI report on the BLS website showed that "food at home" prices rose 0.4% MoM, transportation costs jumped 0.8% MoM, and delivery costs jumped 1.8% MoM. Overall, such pass-through from diesel might make price increases more persistent, with producers baking higher prices into the sale of their goods & services instead of looking through the diesel supply shock as a short-term negative shock to their margins.

- Another piece of evidence that backs this story about pass-through was the October PPI print. Core PPI increased 0.3% MoM, and the October print was the highest YoY PPI increase since 2022. Much of this, again, can be attributed to the Iran diesel supply shock. The primary channel of pass-through, as reported on Bloomberg and quite intuitively, is transportation; this is also a key factor input for all goods and services. With the PPI report showing increasing prices for transportation (2.0% MoM in October, but the base is also higher with how big the growth was in September), inflation today is likely to be worsening. This evidence has led many in the Fed to conclude that a hike in December is justified, and Fed Funds Futures seem to indicate a likely hike.

# Labour Market
Remained the same

- Thursday's initial & continuing claims (for last week & the week before respectively) remained low; 200K & 1697K respectively. Expectations have also anchored themselves to this rough number, so these figures were somewhat closer to expectations compared to past weeks. This is good news for the labour market, as I have written in many past MJs; the initial and continuing claims numbers are at historical lows. However, this trend has continued for many months now, and we have for quite some time been in this equilibrium of low firing & low prevalence of unemployment benefits claims. With that, I would be hard pressed to say that the labour market has improved. At best, it remains the same.

- My case for the labour market remaining the same is also bolstered by the sub-index results of the Employment Trends Index. The ETI, a leading indicator composed of eight sub-indices and a survey-based measure showing the breadth of firms' health on a range of labour market characteristics, edged down in October on a month-over-month basis. The headline print is less interesting than the sub-indices. The ETI reported that more firms indicated having positions they were unable to fill — 36% compared to 32%, indicating stronger demand. The report suggested that this could be because of robust demand, though I wonder whether another possible reason is more reluctance to hire new workers & potential skill incompatibility between workers and job openings. In any case, even if my speculation is wrong, this upside of labour market demand (which could lead to lower unemployment) is neutralized by a potential downside risk of more and more employees being employed in involuntary part-time roles; the proportion of involuntarily part-time individuals in the pool of part-time employees increased to 17.6% from 16.5%. This could indicate that even if labour demand is strong, it isn't necessarily manifesting in currently unemployed people gaining full-time employment. Rather, it seems likely that they might be employed part-time instead. One potential risk in my analysis is if the labour pools for workers who get part-time jobs and full-time jobs are separate, with labour market tightness for the full-time pool and slack for the part-time pool, in which case a higher proportion of part-time workers and an increase in labour demand can co-exist. Even if this risk is true, I think it still indicates that the labour market is no better than previous weeks in that unemployment is likely still as low (and no lower) but will not go up as well.
