# Output
**Remained the same**

- Retail Sales for October slowed down in nominal and headline terms. Ordinarily, one might think this is a cause for concern. However, when accounting for the "control group" of retail sales, it actually grew 0.6% MoM, up from 0.5% the last month. Control group matters because this figure aggregates change in the retail sales categories that go into the formula for GDP. The slowdown in headline retail sales was because of a decline in car and other big ticket item sales, but these are volatile anyways and are a noisier indicator of consumer spending. The strength in control group and online spending points to continued strength in consumer spending on goods. Coupled with strong spending on services we have seen in other indicators (which are not captured in retail sales), consumer spending — the biggest category in GDP = C+I+G+NX — remains robust. That said, the retail sales report provides no evidence that output is any stronger than it previously was.

- Industrial production decreased 0.4% (negative!) MoM. On the surface, this looks like bad news for GDP. However, digging into the reasons for declining industrial production, the decline was because of a weather-related correction in the utilities sector, as well as a slowdown in mining due to Hurricane Isaias disrupting offshore oil extraction. While these two dips contributed to a red number in Industrial Production, this was mitigated by improved factory output, up 0.3% MoM. Bloomberg reported that production of computer and electronic products advanced the most in three months, while production of motor vehicles and parts increased 4.4% — the biggest increase since February 2025. Squaring these two (seemingly contradictory) stories, I believe that while output was slightly worse over the last week, it is offset by strong underlying strength in output that will likely correct output growth back to the positive trajectory we observed in weeks past.

# Labour
**Improved**

- My argument for an improved labour market relies on a stable national picture and hints of good news in the Philly Fed district, which could connote national strength in coming months. Initial jobless claims claims numbers increased by 1k to 203k. The four-week moving average of claims, which irons out week-to-week volatility, increased 2,000 to 197,750. This increase is not a cause for alarm for two reasons. Firstly, this number remains historically low, as I've argued in many past MacroJournals. Declining immigration to the US, as well as high corporate profits, have shielded workers from layoffs, which then results in a historically low number of people filing for unemployment benefits. Secondly, specific to this last week's level change, Reuters reported that the state of Michigan rolled out an online unemployment benefits claims platform, which might have reduced the friction to someone seeking to file unemployment benefits; this caused an increase of 6k people in Michigan filing for benefits. With this in mind, perhaps the increase in jobless claims doesn't connote a worsening in layoffs as much as it reveals a closer approximation of people not in the workforce. Nevertheless, the change compared to last week is not significant.

- The Philly Fed MBOS survey revealed manufacturing businesses' strengthened hiring. The diffusion index (increased - decreased) for number of employees went up from 9.1 to 11.6, showing that a broader range of businesses are now hiring more workers. Not only did businesses hire more people, but more businesses also lengthened existing workers' work weeks — the diffusion index was at 11.2, above last month's 7.5. This reveals a labour market characterized by strong labour demand. The strong labour demand seems to be a long-term trend, with more businesses also confident about further increasing work weeks in the coming six months. The diffusion index for hiring new workers fell this month; my intuition for why is the general trend of low labour availability that we have seen in the weeks past. In any case, labour demand is strong and evidence from this week revealed further strength, implying a low risk of an unemployment shock. One risk to keep in mind in a high demand labour market is a wage-price spiral. That said, there is no evidence that we are in such a spiral; in fact, supply-side inflation shocks have decreased real wages. Overall, thus, the labour market is strong.

# Prices
**Remained the same**

- The inflation expectations print in this month's Michigan Survey of Consumers came in at 3.7%, below last month's 4.7%. The Michigan survey should be taken with a pinch of salt — many respondents are not necessarily trained economists who should be relied on for an accurate read on current inflation. Perhaps more relevant than the absolute level print is the level change, -1.0%. I am skeptical to argue that cooling inflation expectations will douse cold water on currently hot inflation numbers. Proponents of this argument would rely on the fact that inflation expectations influence the "self-fulfilling prophecy" part of the PC curve in the IS-MP-PC three equations model. They would argue that inflation expectations decline, consumers feel no urge to spend now rather than in the future expecting prices to come down. I don't think this argument is very useful today, because the source of inflation is a supply shock in the energy/diesel markets and/or strong investment due to AI capex. Changing inflation expectations, which doesn't seem to be a major cause of inflation, won't do much to address these root causes. Consumers simply seem to be spending in spite of inflation (what one Bloomberg piece called "shrugflation") rather than spending more because of inflation. Additionally, even though this month's expectations print came one percentage point lower, the three month moving average remains elevated compared to the pre-war print.

- Diesel prices cooled a little, coming down to $5.93 a gallon on Thursday. This is lower than the record high September print of $6.53. Energy Secretary Chris Wright argued that the flow of unsanctioned Russian diesel in the global market has put downward pressure on global refined oil prices, which percolates into declining diesel prices in the US even though no Russian diesel shipments have been received in the US. Yet, even as Secretary Wright argued, this requires that "nothing big or bad" happen. This cooling in diesel prices is not quite a result of underlying improvements in the energy markets' capacity to supply refined products. I would argue that there is not yet sufficient evidence that the supply shock is easing. Additionally, even though the point of MJ is to assess changes in one calendar week, it is important to note that $5.93/gallon is far higher than prices from one year ago, $3.80. Diesel prices thus remain a big epsilon-IS shock in our three equations PC curve, which means that inflation today remains far elevated above the Fed's 2% target.
