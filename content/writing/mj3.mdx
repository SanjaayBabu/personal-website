# OUTPUT
- IMPROVED.
- GDP Quarter-over-Quarter SAAR figure for Q2 2026 was revised upwards sharply from 1.5% to 2.2%. This was 70bp higher than forecasters' expectations as well. Upward revisions were made in Consumption, Investment, and Government Spending. The American consumer continues to show exceptional strength, with real consumer spending having increased to 3.8% (annualized) in Q2 2026 — this was a big surge from the far smaller 0.7% in Q1. Given that this is expressed in real terms, such a spike is attributed to increased quantity consumed and not just increased dollar amounts spent due to inflation. As for investment, private inventories and gross fixed investment numbers were strong, with commercial, healthcare & technology infrastructure showing strong gains (with data centers being a stand-out category).
- ISM PMI Manufacturing Report contained decent news overall for output now & good news for output in the coming months. The headline PMI figure was 54.5, a -0.1 point change from August, but still reflecting broadly positive overall sentiment for manufacturing businesses. Manufacturers reported broadly positive figures for production, with a diffusion score of 56.7. While this is down from August (58.3), the figure is still well above the score of 52 that PMI argues is generally in line with Federal Reserve metrics for production growth. As for the future outlook for manufacturing businesses, more businesses reported increased new orders (55.3 in September vs. 53.7 in August) and increased backlogs (56.4 in September vs. 51.8 in August). To fulfill new orders and increasingly large backlogs, production is bound to increase; this squares with information from last week's S&P Global report showing how manufacturing businesses were also looking to hire more workers to meet increased manufacturing production needs.  Something to keep in mind for the next PMI report is — will backlogs increase even more, and at that point might they be increasing too much? That would imply that severe supply chain challenges are making production very challenging, posing a downside risk to growth. For now, the fact that this print is accompanied by a broadly positive Production figure means that backlogs are not excessive. 

# PRICES
- WORSENED.
- Headline PCE MoM growth accelerated to 0.3% (~3.66% when annualized), up from 0.1% in July. Core PCE MoM growth also climbed to 0.2% (~2.43% when annualized). PCE Inflation is high (above the Fed's 2% goal) and increasing even when stripping away oil & food as core PCE does. This suggests that perhaps producers might be passing on (or soon will be passing on) higher input costs to consumers, a point which is being looked at particularly carefully given large price surges in diesel. Diesel is a crucial intermediate input for most goods given its importance in transportation within supply chains for most daily goods. The Wall Street Journal reported this last week about how price pressures have severely affected the transportation bits of the goods supply chain, and it would thus be unsurprising if transportation prices went up significantly & were then passed on to consumers. 
- In the ISM PMI report, we saw a sharp incline the number of purchasing managers saying they paid higher prices for their factor inputs. The diffusion score was 77.9, versus 71.1 in August 2026. This reveals that price pressures were already broad based, that is, affecting a range of manufacturers, but are getting even more so; price pressures exist in most types of manufacturing firms, not just the ones where commodity scarcity is placing pressure on input prices (e.g. copper, memory chips). Again, this adds up with the oil & gas and diesel story; these all are crucial factor inputs for all industries, with diesel standing out as an intermediate input. As producers face higher and higher price pressures, they might be forced to pass higher costs on to consumers. Consequently, we can expect price pressures for consumers (reflected in the CPI & PCE figures) to continue. 

# LABOR MARKET
- Remained the SAME. 
- Unemployment rate (U-3) ticked up 10bp to 4.2%. While this denotes an increase in unemployment *rates* (a bad thing), I would argue that this week's jobs report doesn't signal weakness in the labour market for two reasons. First, labour force participation jumped significantly — it increased 20bp from 61.6% to 61.8%. Mechanically, new entrants typically join the labour force as job seekers before finding work, which pushes up the unemployment rate in the short run. Increased labour force participation is actually a good sign for the long run health of the economy, even if it ticks up unemployment in the short run; it reveals previously discouraged workers' confidence in their ability to find employment. Second, even when accounting for the 10bp increase in unemployment, current unemployment rates remain historically low; the current 4.2% print is still significantly below the US Congressional Budget Office's projection of ~4.4% as the noncyclical rate of unemployment, one commonly used indicator for maximum employment. 
- Payroll growth slowed to 29,000 new jobs. While this is markedly lower than the August print of 133,000 new jobs (revised down), I would make the case that this new level of payroll growth is not bad news in itself; economists have argued that the breakeven rate for US employment has moved downwards as a result of the aging population and declining immigration. The US economy does not have to create as many new jobs to keep unemployment stable; the St. Louis Fed estimated the breakeven range as being between 15k and 87k new jobs. After a strong jobs report in August far surpassing this range, a decline in payroll growth is not a severe challenge; and in any case, the 29k figure still falls within this range. If the breakeven argument holds, we should expect unemployment to stay broadly stable, and the data is consistent with this: the modest rise in U-3 is largely explained by the participation jump discussed above, while U-6 actually fell.
