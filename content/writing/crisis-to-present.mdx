After the horrors of the Second World War, we banded together to create the United Nations — an organization which, for its time, was highly advanced. The United Nations found a way to have the world’s five powers sign on, nearly voluntarily, to an arrangement that limits their power. It created a system of global “norms”, with some enforcement capabilties, that ushered in a relatively minimal period of interstate conflict. 

Less than half a century later, we discovered that there was a hole in the ozone layer. The sheer image of a “hole” in the Earth’s protective layer, and the associated short-term harms of UV radiation and so on, prompted the Montreal Protocol to reduce our emissions of Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs), a chemical that causes the destruction of the ozone layer. Now, we’ve arrested and even reversed the damage to the ozone layer.

And in our more recent lifetimes, we faced a once in a century pandemic. Researchers from around the world shared data, and we managed to create a vaccine in record time that arrested us from the worst outcomes of the pandemic. Of course, that response wasn’t perfect — we took way too long in getting vaccines out to the Global South, and there’s something morally questionable about that. But altogether, that was yet another (relatively) successful response to global challenges. 

What’s the common trend? Our current institutions are really good at hunkering down and fixing problems when they come up. And yes, often, we build some defensive infrastructure right after mitigating the worst of crises. 

However, we should not be quick to celebrate how good we are at doing these two things. These successes obscure a bigger **problem**: we’re not great at having the foresight to anticipate problems, and band together to address them proactively. Is there something we can borrow from the crisis playbook to address crises? 

## A taxonomy of crises
Not all crises are created equally, so maybe it’s first useful to think about what different types of crises there are, and break down what it is about these crises that actually allows us to be so decisive in these moments.

And in writing these taxonomy, it becomes clear (to me) that we’re good at dealing with a specific type of crisis. And perhaps it’s features of this type of crisis that we should borrow from.

I fundamentally think the keystone of crises is **harm**: often harm to human beings. (I don’t know if we’re that good at dealing with crises pertaining to other species, so let’s conveniently exclude that from this discussion. I think that brings us to a broader *ordo amoris* debate which is yet another big debate that I should probably write about sometime, given how relevant it is to the present zeitgeist.) 

Within harm, there are some big questions to ask:
- **Actors**: Who is being harmed? Who has the levers to stop the harm? And is there a difference between the two?
- **Time horizon**: When is the harm happening? Does it all come at once, or is it slow burn? 
- **Knowledge**: Do we know what is causing the harm? Is it clearly defined? Do we understand and agree on (most parts of) the causal chain between the cause and the harmful effect?

The crises that we are good at are ones with shared immediate harm, but consequences are too large for one person to manage alone. Pandemics are a clear example. If there is a disease spreading around the world, then to solve the problem in one place, you have to solve it in every place. In this crisis, the players being harmed and the players with the levers of change are one and the same. Additionally, the players know that the harm is happening (because it’s immediate), and we can see feedback loops. As such, assuming we know what to do, change is bound to happen. 

## A crisis solution playbook
So we have a pulse of what we need:
(a) To align the people who have the levers of change and those who are being harmed; 
(b) To treat slow-burn harms as seriously as quick-burn harms; and
(c) Get better at communicating what it is that is causing the harm.
