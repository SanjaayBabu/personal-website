“Can we build societies that are not just functional and stable, but also thriving and happy?” This question drove my 10-month “gap year” between military service and college, during which I traveled across Asia and Europe.

This aspirational question first requires us to look at a more fundamental one: Is politics only the art of the possible, as Otto von Bismarck suggested? Is pragmatism the primary organizing principle of politics?

Indeed, if governments exclusively value pragmatism, they should seek only to create a backdrop for people to live their individual lives, and find their own forms of happiness and satisfaction. This view is Hobbesian and Lockean — government is preferable to the state of nature, not because it inherently adds value, but because it prevents the chaos and brutality of the state of nature. 

Strikingly, this view starkly differs from Plato’s idealistic conception of government. The ideal government, in Plato’s view, should improve people’s reason and virtue, ultimately promoting their well-being. The government is inherently valuable, in this case. It is the sum of individual happiness and more. 

Hobbes and Locke are the ideological godfathers of the modern nation-state, and Plato’s view looks more foreign. Why? Merely that Hobbes’ and Locke’s view is more congruent with modern realities. With large populations, modern weapons and diverse societies, we can’t enjoy the kind of direct democracy that Athens at the time of Plato enjoyed. While pragmatism isn’t the only principle we value, it is certainly the overarching belief today. 

I wonder what a more aspirational conception of government looks like. Hobbes’ skeptical view may be too extreme; as Christopher Wray argues in Civilized to Death, this "man is wolf to man" view was shaped by the chaos of the English Civil War and is a product of nurture rather than nature. A first step toward an aspirational government is greater faith in humanity.

I have some ideas after my travels: 
1. We could bring back some form of direct democracy, not just through majoritarian voting, but also concepts such as Citizens’ Assemblies through Circles (an idea I explored during my coursework on Restorative Justice). 
2. We should bring back discourse about moral questions, rather than aspiring to an almost-impossible amorality — this was Michael 3. Sandel’s argument in Justice: What’s the Right Thing to Do? 
Finally, we need to rebuild national identities, such that citizenship comes not just with rights, but obligations to one another.
